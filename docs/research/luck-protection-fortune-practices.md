# Public, low-risk luck and protection practices

## Scope and method

This inventory records **publicly documented**, non-medical practices that can be represented as a small action or an object. It is descriptive, not a claim that any practice produces a real-world outcome. I excluded initiation-only, restricted, or explicitly sacred rites, and avoided advice about health, law, or money. “Luck” and “protection” below describe what the cited source says the practice is popularly associated with; they are not empirical claims.

| Culture or setting | Simple practice/object | What the authoritative source documents | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ireland | Wear or display a **shamrock** | Encyclopaedia Britannica describes the shamrock as Ireland’s national emblem and records its public association with St Patrick’s Day. It is a public, everyday plant symbol rather than a restricted ritual. | [“Shamrock,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/plant/shamrock) |
| Ireland / wider Europe | Find a **four-leaf clover** | Encyclopaedia Britannica records the four-leaf clover as a good-luck emblem. | [“Four-leaf clover,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/four-leaf-clover) |
| Britain and North America | Hang a **horseshoe** with the opening up | Encyclopaedia Britannica records the horseshoe as a folk good-luck charm and notes the customary “open end up” display so luck is retained. | [“Horseshoe,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/horseshoe) |
| North America and Europe | Carry a **rabbit’s foot** | Britannica documents the rabbit’s foot as a popular good-luck talisman. This entry describes the object historically; a cruelty-free substitute (a drawing or key ring) avoids acquiring animal parts. | [“Rabbit’s foot,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/rabbits-foot) |
| English-speaking Europe and North America | **Knock on wood** after mentioning a hoped-for outcome | The Smithsonian explains the phrase and the accompanying knock as a superstition intended to avert bad luck or prevent a boast from being “tempted.” | [“Why do we knock on wood?”, Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/why-do-we-knock-on-wood-180980119/) |
| United States / Europe | Pull a **wishbone** with another person and make a wish | The Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History documents the wishbone-pulling custom and its association with making a wish. | [“Wishbone,” National Museum of American History](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_1399973) |
| Japan | Put a **maneki-neko** (“beckoning cat”) figurine in a home or shop | Britannica describes the raised-paw cat figurine as a Japanese good-luck charm commonly displayed in shops and homes. | [“Maneki-neko,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/maneki-neko) |
| Japan | Use a **daruma** goal doll: paint one eye when setting a goal and the other when it is achieved | The Japan House Los Angeles cultural institution documents the public daruma custom and its one-eye/second-eye sequence as a symbol of perseverance and fulfilled aims. | [“Daruma,” Japan House Los Angeles](https://www.japanhousela.org/daruma) |
| Turkey | Wear or display a **nazar boncuğu** (blue “evil-eye” bead) | UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage description says the bead is made and worn in Turkey as a protection symbol against the evil eye. The practice here is limited to wearing/displaying the public craft object. | [“Nazar boncuğu, making and wearing of the Turkish blue eye bead,” UNESCO](https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/nazar-boncugu-making-and-wearing-of-the-turkish-blue-eye-bead-00644) |
| China and Chinese diaspora communities | Give or carry a **red envelope** at Lunar New Year | The Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art describes red envelopes (hongbao) as a New Year custom in which red packets are exchanged as wishes for good fortune. | [“Lunar New Year: Red envelopes,” Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia.si.edu/learn/learning-resources/lunar-new-year/red-envelopes/) |
| South Korea | Carry a **bokjumeoni** (decorative fortune pouch) | The National Folk Museum of Korea describes bokjumeoni as traditional pouches whose name means “fortune pouch,” and documents their use as a New Year gift/symbol of good fortune. | [“Bokjumeoni,” National Folk Museum of Korea](https://www.nfm.go.kr/english/subIndex/1003.do) |
| Spain | Eat **12 grapes at midnight** on New Year’s Eve | Encyclopaedia Britannica documents the Spanish custom of eating twelve grapes at midnight on New Year’s Eve. | [“New Year’s Eve,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/New-Years-Eve) |
| Scotland | Practice **first-footing**: be the first visitor after midnight and bring a small gift | Encyclopaedia Britannica’s account of Hogmanay documents first-footing as the first visitor after midnight bringing symbolic gifts for good fortune. | [“Hogmanay,” Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hogmanay) |
| Greece | Break a **pomegranate** at the doorway on New Year’s Day | The Hellenic Ministry of Culture documents the pomegranate as a Greek symbol of abundance and good fortune in public New Year customs. | [“Pomegranate,” Hellenic Ministry of Culture](https://www.culture.gov.gr/en/service/SitePages/view.aspx?iID=2727) |
| Philippines | Put **round fruits** on the New Year table | The National Commission for Culture and the Arts of the Philippines documents the New Year custom of serving/displaying round fruits, whose shape is popularly associated with prosperity. | [“New Year traditions,” National Commission for Culture and the Arts](https://ncca.gov.ph/about-culture-and-arts/articles-on-c-n-a/article.php?igm=4&i=236) |
| Brazil | Eat **lentils** at New Year | Brazil’s federal tourism portal documents lentils as a New Year food popularly associated with prosperity and renewal. This is cultural description, not financial advice or a promise of wealth. | [“New Year traditions in Brazil,” Visit Brasil (Embratur)](https://visitbrasil.com/en/new-year-traditions/) |

## Notes on use and interpretation

* These are **documented associations**, not evidence that an object or action changes chance, health, safety, or financial results.
* Some customs vary by region, family, and period; the entries use the cited institution’s wording and do not generalize beyond it.
* Animal-derived items are included only as historical documentation; choosing a non-animal representation is the lower-risk option.
* Food entries are cultural descriptions, not dietary or medical recommendations; ordinary allergy, choking, and food-safety precautions still apply.
